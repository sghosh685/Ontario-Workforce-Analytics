# Ontario Workforce Snapshot, 2021-2026

## Policy Brief

### Purpose

This brief summarizes changes in Ontario's labour market between January 2021 and April 2026 using Statistics Canada monthly labour force and industry employment data. The goal is to identify sectors showing sustained employment growth, sectors showing recent softness, and implications for workforce planning.

### Headline Finding

Ontario employment is above its 2021 level, but the sector picture is uneven. Long-run growth is concentrated in health care, professional services, accommodation and food, retail, and information/culture/recreation. The latest year-over-year picture is more mixed, with transportation and health care gaining while education, public administration, professional services, accommodation and food, and manufacturing show declines.

### Current Labour Market Context

As of April 2026:

- Ontario employment was **8,247.9 thousand**, or about **8.25 million people**.
- Ontario's unemployment rate was **7.5%**.
- The labour force was **8,920.8 thousand**, or about **8.92 million people**.
- The participation rate was **64.8%**.
- Employment increased **54.3 thousand** compared with April 2025, a **0.7%** year-over-year increase.

### Sector Findings

The strongest employment increases since January 2021 were:

- Health care and social assistance: **+176.0 thousand**
- Professional, scientific and technical services: **+161.5 thousand**
- Accommodation and food services: **+114.9 thousand**
- Wholesale and retail trade: **+109.9 thousand**
- Information, culture and recreation: **+107.7 thousand**

The strongest latest year-over-year increases were:

- Transportation and warehousing: **+40.6 thousand**
- Health care and social assistance: **+39.7 thousand**
- Information, culture and recreation: **+23.7 thousand**
- Business, building and other support services: **+16.4 thousand**
- Other services except public administration: **+10.1 thousand**

The largest latest year-over-year declines were:

- Educational services: **-32.9 thousand**
- Public administration: **-12.9 thousand**
- Professional, scientific and technical services: **-12.5 thousand**
- Accommodation and food services: **-12.0 thousand**
- Manufacturing: **-8.5 thousand**

### Implications

First, health care and social assistance stands out as both a long-run growth sector and a latest year-over-year growth sector. That supports continued attention to training capacity, retention, credential pathways, and workforce pipeline planning.

Second, professional services has strong long-run gains but a recent year-over-year decline. This suggests the sector should not be interpreted only through its post-2021 growth. It may require monitoring alongside broader economic conditions, hiring demand, and business investment.

Third, education and public administration show recent year-over-year softness. These sectors are important for public service capacity and should be monitored before drawing conclusions from one month of data.

Fourth, transportation and warehousing leads the latest year-over-year growth. This may be relevant for supply chain, logistics, infrastructure, and regional workforce planning discussions.

### Recommended Next Analysis

The next version should add either occupation or region. Occupation would strengthen the project for labour market analyst roles because it connects sector change to skill demand. Region would strengthen the project for workforce planning because it shows whether pressure is concentrated in specific Ontario labour markets.

Recommended additions:

- Occupation-level employment or job vacancy data
- Ontario region or census metropolitan area breakdowns
- Job Bank or Ontario labour market report context
- A short employer-demand comparison using job postings or vacancy data

### Methodology

The analysis uses two Statistics Canada tables:

- Table 14-10-0287-01 for monthly labour force characteristics
- Table 14-10-0355-01 for monthly employment by industry

Both files were filtered to Ontario, estimate rows, and seasonally adjusted values. Labour indicators were reshaped into one monthly row per period. Industry summaries exclude aggregate rollups when ranking sectors.

### Limitations

This project is descriptive. It does not prove why employment changed. Monthly labour market movements can reflect seasonal adjustment, sampling variability, population growth, macroeconomic conditions, public policy, and industry-specific factors. The dashboard should be used as a screening and briefing tool, not as a causal evaluation.
